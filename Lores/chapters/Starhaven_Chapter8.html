I. Iron and Archive

Forgehaven · Vaelmark · Year 1047

You heard Forgehaven before you saw it.

The sound traveled further than it should — carried on the wind in the specific way of industrial sound, the deep percussion of forge hammers and the rhythmic exhale of smelter bellows and beneath both of these, constant and foundational, the subsonic resonance of furnaces that had been burning without pause since the day the first stone of the Tidegate Arsenal was laid.

The furnaces never cooled.

This was not hyperbole or civic pride speaking.

It was the operational requirement of a city whose entire purpose was to produce things that required sustained heat to produce.

Shutting a Forgehaven furnace down was not a rest — it was a decision with a two-week recovery cost while the materials equilibrated to operating temperature again.

Nobody shut them down.

The city never slept.

The approach from the Starhold road gave you the skyline first.

Not the elegant skyline of Vaelmark's crystal towers catching mountain light, or Starhold's administrative spires that had been designed with the future in mind.

Forgehaven's skyline was functional.

Forge stacks rising at intervals across the industrial district, each one trailing its specific combination of heat-shimmer and metal-smoke that caught the morning light in amber and grey depending on what was being processed below.

The Tidegate Arsenal's main complex rising behind the outer wall — the forge halls' peaked rooflines, the foundry buildings' broader shoulders, the long flat profile of the tank maintenance bay that was, by square footage, the largest single-purpose structure in the kingdom.

And above everything, visible from six miles at the right angle: the railway terminus.

The Forgehaven Iron Yards.

Three tracks of Cyrus Vexley's arcane railway terminating at the platform complex that served both the Arsenal's logistics needs and the city's civilian connections — the specific hub that linked Forgehaven to Starhold to the east, to the expanding network of lines that had been building since Year 1044 and that made the industrial capital's output available to every city in the kingdom within a day's transit time.

The Iron Yards ran at full capacity every hour of every shift.

What came in: ore, raw materials, the specific inputs that Forgehaven's processes consumed in quantities that no road network could have sustained.

What went out: Iron Tide tanks in components for final assembly at forward positions, siege weapon systems in transit to Vera Holt's battery emplacements, augmented soldier plating in sealed crates for the Augmented Battalion's maintenance program, and the ordinary production of a city that also, alongside everything else, made things for the kingdom's civilian population because twenty thousand industrial workers required goods and services and trade, and trade moved by rail.

Forgehaven had been built from a mineral deposit.

This was not the official founding myth of the city — it had no official founding myth, because the people who built it were not the kind of people who spent time on myths when there was work to be done.

But it was the accurate account.

In Year 1044, during the survey work that followed the founding year's stabilization, Kael Varric had identified the geological formation beneath the central plains as the largest untouched mineral deposit in the kingdom's territory.

Not corrupted.

Not Void-touched.

Simply undiscovered — sitting beneath unremarkable terrain that had been unremarkable for a long time and had been waiting, with the patience of geological formations, for someone to notice it.

Kael's report to the council: three sentences and a map annotation.

"Forgehaven sits on the largest untouched mineral deposit in the kingdom. It was not corrupted. We should build an industrial city."

They had built one.

In Year 1047, the city's population had crossed eighty thousand — a number that would have seemed implausible for a settlement that had not existed five years ago if the visible evidence had not made implausibility difficult to sustain.

The Arsenal district alone: twelve thousand workers across six forge halls, four foundries, two materials processing facilities, the experimental fabrication workshop, and the ongoing eastern expansion that had been under construction for six months and would be completed before winter.

The civilian quarter behind the Arsenal: the city that had grown to support the Arsenal's population, with the specific organic logic of a place that had never been designed from a city planner's perspective but had been assembled from the requirements of people who needed somewhere to live and eat and send their children and argue and drink, and had built accordingly.

The Tidegate Harbor at the city's western edge: the waterfront facility that gave the Arsenal its name — the original loading gate for Forgehaven's manufactured output, where Iron Legion equipment was shipped and supply chain logistics for the western theater were managed from docks that ran the same hours as the furnaces.

The Tidegate Arsenal itself was the city's heart, in the same way that a heart was a heart — not because it was the most visible part of the organism, but because when it stopped everything else stopped with it.

Cain Drakhar's Design Hall on the Arsenal's northern face — the personal workspace where the problems that could not be solved on the main floor were brought, and where the answers that had not yet been built were assembled from first principles before they became the things the rest of the Arsenal manufactured.

The Blacksteel Foundry adjacent: the facility that produced the specific dense iron alloy that Cain had specified for every piece of Iron Legion plate — not standard military grade, not the formulation that met the minimum requirements of the specification, but the formulation that actually did what the specification was trying to achieve.

The 280-Tank Maintenance Bay stretching the Arsenal's southern length: operational capacity for the full Iron Tide fleet simultaneously, the largest single-purpose structure in the kingdom by square footage — a fact that visitors registered and then went very quiet about for a moment as they understood what the size implied about the scale of what the bay was built to contain.

The Ballistic Testing Range on the eastern grounds: operating daily, feeding data back to the Design Hall, and from the Design Hall into the next generation of what was being designed.

And at the Arsenal's center, accessible through a door with no identifying markings that most Arsenal workers had never been through: Cain Drakhar's personal workshop.

Restricted.

Not because the work there was politically sensitive — Cain Drakhar had no patience for political sensitivity and it had developed no relationship with his workflow accordingly.

Restricted because some work required conditions that twelve thousand workers generating forge-hall noise across six adjacent buildings could not provide unless the specific door was between him and them.

It was a good door.

Two rift gates served the city — one in the Arsenal's logistics yard for direct material and equipment transit, one in the city's civilian district for the population that lived and worked there and maintained the specific quality of a city that had never pretended to be anything other than what it was.

What it was:

The industrial heart of the kingdom.

Where iron became the kingdom's fist.

The city that never slept because the furnaces never cooled because the work never stopped.

In Year 1047, Cain Drakhar arrived to build it into something else.

He arrived in the morning, before the first shift.

The Tidegate Arsenal's main gate was open.

It was always open.

Not because the city lacked walls — Forgehaven had walls, good ones, built from the same dense grey stone that Kael had specified for every major Starhaven installation and that had the specific quality of things built to outlast the problems that made them necessary.

The gate was open because Forgehaven had never found a reason to close it.

The city's defense was the city itself — what it produced, what it housed, the specific things that anyone who opened that gate aggressively would immediately regret.

Cain walked through it without stopping.

No ceremony.

No escort beyond the two Dawnguard soldiers who had accompanied him from Dawncliff and who fell back to a respectful distance the moment he passed the threshold, understanding — correctly — that their function in this specific city was different from their function elsewhere.

He stood inside the main gate for thirty seconds.

Not looking at the city the way visitors looked at it — with the eyes of someone receiving an impression, building an aesthetic.

With the eyes of someone performing an assessment.

Forgehaven in Year 1047 was already significant.

The Arsenal's main complex: six forge halls, four foundries, two materials processing facilities, one experimental fabrication workshop that Velkan had funded in Year 1044 and that had been producing results at a rate that justified the funding and then the funding that followed the funding and then the building that was currently being added to its eastern side.

The factory district behind the Arsenal: manufacturing infrastructure that had expanded every year since the kingdom's founding, each expansion absorbing the previous one's capacity and exceeding it.

The workers: twelve thousand in the Arsenal alone, another eight thousand in the surrounding industrial district, the specific population of a city that had built itself around a single organizing principle — that making things was the work, and making them correctly was the only standard that mattered.

Cain looked at all of it.

Saw what it was.

Saw what it needed.

Began walking toward the main forge hall.

The foreman — a man named Orren who had been running the main hall since Year 1043 and who had been warned that the new Lord of Forgehaven would arrive sometime this week but had not been told which day — was reviewing the morning production numbers when the door to his office opened without a knock.

He looked up.

The person in his doorway was more metal than man.

This was something Orren had known abstractly from the descriptions that preceded the arrival.

Knowing it abstractly and encountering it directly were, he discovered in that specific moment, two different experiences.

Cain looked at the production numbers on Orren's desk.

Then at the forge hall visible through the office's window.

"The third smelter."

Orren blinked.

"The feed angle is wrong."

"Twelve degrees when it should be fourteen."

"It's costing you approximately three hundred pounds of yield per day in waste impurities."

He looked at Orren.

"Show me the maintenance records."

Orren showed him the maintenance records.

The feed angle had been twelve degrees since Year 1044.

Three hundred pounds per day for three years.

Cain looked at the numbers.

Did not perform the calculation aloud because Orren could do it himself.

"Fix it this afternoon."

He closed the maintenance record and left the office.

Orren, in the empty office, sat with the specific experience of a man who has been running a facility for four years and has just encountered someone who identified its primary efficiency problem in thirty seconds through a window.

He fixed the feed angle that afternoon.

He also pulled every other maintenance record in the hall and reviewed them that evening.

He found six more problems.

He fixed those too.

Cain's lordship was, in structure, the only lordship he had a model for — which was no lordship at all, simply the application of functional authority to functional problems.

He did not hold court.

He did not receive delegations in a formal hall.

He did not issue proclamations.

What he did: he walked the facility every morning at first light before the day shifts began, identifying what needed changing, and by the time the first workers arrived the changes were already in progress.

The twelve thousand people in the Arsenal understood the arrangement within the first two weeks.

Not through explanation.

Through the specific pattern of waking up to find that the problem they had been working around for six months had been solved overnight and that solving it had apparently not required a committee or a proposal or a formal review.

It had required someone to notice it and act on it.

The thing about Cain Drakhar's noticing — as the Arsenal's foremen and shift supervisors discovered in those first weeks — was that it was comprehensive.

The feed angle on the third smelter.

The load distribution in the secondary foundry that was creating stress fractures in the floor plates.

The ventilation inadequacy in Fabrication Hall Four that had been causing the afternoon shift workers to develop the specific headache pattern of mild metal particulate exposure.

The materials routing system whose current layout moved product three hundred unnecessary meters per batch through the complex.

He fixed all of it.

Not because he was performing lordship.

Because broken things needed fixing and he was in the building.

Employment expanded not because Cain issued an employment policy but because the work that the archive was beginning to generate required more hands than currently existed, and more hands required training, and he trained them.

Personally.

Not all of them — he was one person, with a limited number of hours, and the archive contained a quantity of knowledge that would require years to fully deploy.

But the senior fabricators, the lead engineers, the foremen who were going to implement the new processes — he taught them directly.

Not enthusiastically.

Completely.

Incompetence had always offended him on a technical level.

Incompetence that resulted from inadequate instruction was specifically wasteful in the way that preventable problems were wasteful — the fault was in the system, not the person, and the system was his to fix.

He taught them.

They learned.

The facility's output began changing in the second month and did not stop changing.

The archive's first practical applications were not dramatic.

They were not announced.

There was no demonstration, no moment of revealed capability, no ceremonial first production run of the improved design.

The pre-Sundering people had faced this problem and solved it three hundred years ago.

The solution had been in the archive.

Cain had been building toward it instinctively since Year 1038 when he had first built something with metal and discovered that the metal told him things if he paid attention to what it was doing.

He looked at the completed chassis.

Did not perform satisfaction in any visible way.

Walked around it once.

Confirmed what he already knew.

Made three notations in the production log.

Moved to the next problem.

Orren watched him do this.

Later — much later — he would describe the experience to his wife over dinner in the specific compressed way of men who process significant experiences through mundane conversation:

He looked at the thing he'd been building for twenty years and found the answer he'd been building toward, and then he went to fix the intake valve on the secondary foundry.

His wife asked: Was he happy?

Orren thought about this.

I think he was functional.

He paused.

I think those are the same thing for him.

All of them.

Three times.

Not because the Stonethrow Corps' hybrid Vaelmark-Havenfire artillery system was relevant to what he was building at Forgehaven — it was, but that was a secondary consideration.

Because the design reports revealed, in the specific way that technical documents revealed the quality of the mind behind them, that Vera Holt understood siege engineering the way he understood manufacturing — not as a discipline with defined solutions but as a set of problems that required finding the correct answer rather than the nearest available one.

Vera Holt arrived from Starhold in the seventh week.

She came through the Forgehaven rift gate with two crates of siege design documentation, four notebooks she was actively using, and the particular expression of someone who has been waiting for a specific thing to happen and has stopped waiting because it has happened.

She was twenty-eight years old. She had built her first siege weapon at Havenfire — not as a title, not as an assignment, but because there was a dwarven heavy crossbow mounted on a swivel brace above the gate that required two people to draw and she was the only one standing next to it. The bolt punched through three corrupted bodies and buried itself in the mud beyond and she immediately shouted down to the forge dwarves to build more. They did. She had spent the night before the Starhold march working alongside those same dwarves — building twenty-five siege machines by torchlight while the rest of Havenfire slept, loading them onto carriages, checking every bolt and mechanism and load balance against road weight calculations she had written and revised twice before she was satisfied. She had walked the column anyway when everything was already done. On the march to Starhold she had spotted a stress fracture in a wheel axle mid-descent that would have sent an arcane cannon platform into the ravine below. "The barrel alignment matters very little," she had told the dwarven engineer who protested, "if the entire machine falls into the forest." The dwarves had obeyed immediately. At the battle she had positioned the coalition artillery, directed the siege crews through mud and enemy fire without pause, and when a shell destroyed a platform and the stabilizer was gone she had climbed onto it through falling debris and grabbed the firing lever herself. "Then we fire before the platform collapses." The shell hit Starhold's western tower batteries. She had not waited for a title at Havenfire. She had not waited for permission at Starhold. The title had followed the work — always after, never before

Not because he had deprioritized them — because siege engineering was not his primary domain and the archive's siege sections required someone who understood the problem space from the inside, who would read what the pre-Sundering engineers had solved and immediately understand why the solution mattered and what it changed.

Then at the Stonethrow Corps' hybrid system documentation she had brought in the crates.

The rift gate architecture.

Velkan's theoretical framework for the pre-Sundering knowledge:

The archive was not simply a catalogue of specific technical solutions.

Systems that expanded.

The second visit, three months later: the Drake rider harnesses.

A different problem — not materials inefficiency but structural design, the harness attachment points using a geometry that distributed the mechanical load across fewer contact surfaces than the pre-Sundering equivalent had used.

At current operational speeds and altitudes, the difference was acceptable.

At the speeds and altitudes that the improved staves would allow the Corps to operate at, the difference was not.

He identified this before anyone else had considered the second-order effects of the stave improvement.

Filed the harness redesign in his work queue.

Scheduled it for the month after the stave program completed.

Told no one except Sorin and Velkan, who needed to know for operational planning purposes.

This was also how Cain worked.

The current improvement implied future improvements, and the future improvements needed to be designed before the current ones were deployed, and the designing was simply another item in the queue, and the queue was always full, and full queues were not a problem — they were evidence that the work was correctly scoped.

The augmentation work happened in the sixth month, during the nights.

Not dramatically.

Not announced.

The forge hall's eastern workspace, which Cain kept locked during off-hours for work that required conditions he could not maintain with other people present, was occupied between the second and fourth hours of the morning for eleven consecutive nights.

What the archive had revealed about augmentation integration — the pre-Sundering techniques for interfacing metal components with biological tissue at tolerances significantly tighter than the current methods allowed — was not the most urgent application of the archive's knowledge.

It was not what Cain had prioritized when he organized the archive by deployment order.

But the Vrak Isles had moved the augmentation substantially further than it had been before the three years there.

Some of that work had been done under conditions of urgency — components replaced with what was available rather than what was optimal, integrations completed without the refinement that optimal integration required.

The archive had the refinement.

The forge had the materials.

The nights were quiet.

He refined eleven integration points over eleven nights.

Not modifications.

Corrections — bringing the existing work up to the standard the archive indicated was achievable and that the Vrak Isles' conditions had not permitted.

The result was not visible from the outside in any obvious way.

The operational improvements were in the efficiency of the interface between the biological components that remained and the metal components that had replaced others — the specific quality of systems that communicated with each other correctly rather than adequately.

He filed it in his maintenance log under routine augmentation maintenance.

The entry was three lines.

On the twelfth morning, Seraphine was visiting Forgehaven for a combined purification protocol review with the Ashen Guard's western deployment coordination.

She came through the Arsenal's main gate, walked through the forge hall complex, and passed the eastern workspace where the augmentation work had been happening.

She stopped.

Not at the door — at the point in the corridor where the specific frequency of the pre-Sundering integration work's arcane residue was noticeable to someone whose perception of arcane signatures was what hers was.

She stood there for a moment.

Then continued toward the coordination meeting.

That afternoon, passing the fabrication workshop where Cain was working, she looked at him once.

He looked back.

Neither of them said anything.

The understanding between them did not require anything to be said.

She had brought him back from the end on the deck of the Permanent Tide.

He had thanked her with two words that had not previously had a protocol.

He was using what she had given him to build something he had been building toward since before either of them had known the other existed.

That was the whole of it.

That was enough.

In the eighth month, Cain brought Cyrus a set of design requirements.

This happened in the evening, after the day's work, when the fabrication workshop was quiet and the other members of their working group had either returned to Vaelmark through the rift gate or were occupied elsewhere in the complex.

He placed the documents on the central table without explanation.

Cyrus read them.

He read them with the same focused attention he gave to every engineering specification, which was: comprehensively, in order, without skipping ahead to the summary when the details were the point.

He finished the first page.

Read it again.

Then continued.

When he finished the last page he set the documents on the table and was quiet for a moment.

"This is a vessel that goes down."

"Yes."

"How far down."

Cain told him.

The third island chain of the Vrak Isles.

The specific depth at which the workshop and the tank and everything he had built there in three years now rested on the ocean floor — the depth at which the Void-saturated substrate had finally given way and taken the third chain's interior down with it.

The depth at which the archive's notation at the top of the lost-items index was waiting.

For retrieval.

Cyrus looked at the design requirements.

At the pressure ratings, the materials specifications, the operational depth range, the specific engineering problems that a vessel designed to reach that depth in that environment would need to solve.

He was quiet for a longer moment.

Not because the problem was impossible.

Because he was doing the honest preliminary assessment of a man who had built six railway lines and thirty-one rift gate platforms and the Forgehaven-to-Starhold terminus and had learned that honest preliminary assessment was the thing that separated work that succeeded from work that failed expensively.

"That's not impossible."

"I know."

"I need four months before I can tell you it's possible in the specific way it needs to be possible."

"Four months for the feasibility assessment."

"Then the design."

"Then the build."

"Yes."

Cyrus looked at the requirements again.

The pressure hull geometry.

The depth-rated material specifications that the pre-Sundering archive had made possible where nothing in current engineering knowledge could have achieved them.

The propulsion system that was, in its fundamental architecture, a variation of the same magnetic principle the railway used — extended from two dimensions into three, applied to a medium that was not stone and steel but saltwater and the specific pressure of several hundred meters of ocean above it.

He picked up his pen.

Turned to a new page in the engineering notebook he always had open when he was working.

"The pressure hull is the problem."

"I know."

"What does the archive say about it."

Cain turned to the relevant archive section in the notebook he always had open when he was working.

They worked until the second hour of the morning.

When Orren arrived for the first shift, the central table had sixteen pages of preliminary calculations on it and both men were gone — Cain to his augmentation work space, Cyrus to the southern room and his notebooks and the specific problem that he had just accepted into his queue.

The vessel that goes down.

Four months.

Then the rest of it.
