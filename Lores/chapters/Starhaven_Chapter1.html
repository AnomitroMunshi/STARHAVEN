The first winter Rayden Cale remembered smelled of wet ash.

Not snow.

Not pine.

Ash.

Even years later, long after kingdoms fell beneath his banners and armies crossed oceans under Starhaven colors, smoke still reached him first whenever memory opened old doors.

Smoke.

Then screaming.

Then fire.

That was how his life began.

I. Oaksmane Orphanage

Sundered Age — Year 1031

Before kingdoms.

Before Starhaven.

Before men called him a legend.

Rayden Cale belonged to a collapsing orphanage in the lower districts of Oaksmane.

Oaksmane was the kind of place wealthy people described as “necessary.”

Factories stood beside brothels.

Churches beside gambling dens.

Children beside corpses.

The rich districts uphill called it the labor quarter.

The people living there called it survival.

Smoke permanently stained the sky above the district from iron foundries and tannery furnaces. The narrow canals running between the streets carried slaughterhouse runoff dark enough to resemble watered blood during winter.

Most people born in Oaksmane never escaped it.

Most died owing someone money.

The orphanage stood near the canal district behind an abandoned grain warehouse and leaned slightly eastward from old structural damage nobody could afford to repair.

The roof leaked during storms.

The cellar flooded every spring.

The windows froze from the inside during winter.

But inside those walls, thirty-two abandoned children still found something resembling home.

That alone made the building more valuable than half the noble estates in Oaksmane.

Sister Marianne ran the orphanage with exhaustion hidden behind discipline.

She taught reading every morning using damaged books donated by churches.

Math lessons came after lunch.

Chores before dusk.

The older children hauled coal and water.

The younger ones cleaned floors, repaired blankets, peeled vegetables, and patched clothes near the stove.

Every candle mattered.

Every loaf of bread mattered.

Every winter felt dangerous.

But Marianne refused to allow the children to become feral.

That stubbornness kept many of them alive.

Sister Marianne · Every Letter Is a Door

Rayden was eleven years old that winter.

Too angry for his age.

Too observant.

Too willing to fight.

He spent most mornings bruised from arguments with older boys or bleeding from training accidents in the yard behind the orphanage.

His closest friend was another orphan named Caelum, a sharp-tongued boy two years older than him who possessed unstable magical talent he barely understood.

Sometimes candles lit when Caelum lost his temper.

Sometimes spoons bent during arguments.

Once, during winter, the wash basin froze solid because another boy punched him hard enough to split his lip.

The other children thought it was incredible.

Rayden thought it meant Caelum would survive longer than most people in Oaksmane.

The two became inseparable.

They stole bread from the dock markets together.

Shared blankets during winter freezes.

Fought older street gangs behind warehouse alleys.

At night they whispered about impossible futures while rain leaked through the ceiling overhead.

Caelum wanted to become a mage someday.

Rayden wanted enough strength that nobody could ever make children afraid again.

Neither boy understood how expensive those dreams truly were.

Rayden’s older brother, Garrick Cale, returned whenever military rotations allowed.

To the children, Garrick felt larger than ordinary men.

Not because he was famous.

Because he cared.

He fixed broken furniture.

Brought extra food whenever he could steal military rations safely.

Remembered birthdays.

Stayed awake beside children with nightmares.

In Oaksmane, kindness looked almost supernatural.

But Garrick carried something heavy behind his eyes.

Rayden noticed it more each year.

The exhaustion.

The restrained anger.

The constant tension whenever certain names appeared in conversation.

Because Garrick did not serve a kingdom.

He served under Lord Halbrecht Veyne’s district military authority.

Halbrecht controlled most military contracts surrounding Oaksmane and the lower industrial territories.

Poor boys became soldiers beneath him because poor boys needed food more than dignity.

Garrick hated the work.

Marianne hated it more.

But military pay helped keep the orphanage alive during famine winters.

That was how men like Halbrecht ruled places like Oaksmane.

Not through loyalty.

Dependency.

Whenever Garrick returned, training began immediately.

The older boys gathered behind the orphanage beside the frozen canal carrying wooden practice swords carved from scrap lumber.

Even some girls joined despite Garrick pretending not to notice.

Marianne watched from the back steps every time while pretending to organize laundry.

Usually failing.

Garrick taught discipline before combat.

“A sword doesn’t make you dangerous,”

he told them repeatedly.

“Hunger does. Fear does. A sword only decides what kind of monster you become afterward.”

Most children only half-understood lessons like that.

Rayden listened to every word.

Garrick trained Rayden harder than the others.

Longer stances.

More punishment drills.

More bruises.

One freezing evening after practice, Garrick stopped Rayden mid-swing beside the canal wall.

“You’re fighting angry again.”

Rayden tightened both hands around the wooden sword.

“So?”

Garrick looked toward the factory smoke beyond Oaksmane.

“So angry men die early.”

Rayden frowned.

“What survives then?”

For a while Garrick said nothing.

Then quietly:

“The ones who know what they’re protecting.”

Garrick Cale · The Ones Who Know What They’re Protecting

Marianne and Garrick spent long nights together after the children slept.

Usually near the kitchen stove beneath dim candlelight.

Repairing clothes.

Reviewing ration books.

Speaking quietly while snow gathered outside the windows.

The children pretended not to notice the way Marianne smiled differently around him.

Or how Garrick relaxed slightly whenever she entered the room.

But everyone noticed.

Especially Rayden.

Sometimes he woke during the night and saw them sitting together silently with their hands touching across the table beside the dying stove fire.

Not speaking.

Just existing beside each other in peace for a few stolen minutes.

In Oaksmane, that counted as luxury.

Marianne & Garrick · The Evening He Came Back

Fear always lived near the orphanage.

Especially after dark.

Some nights expensive carriages moved too slowly through the district streets.

Some guards stared too long at older girls.

Some wealthy men donated food with smiles that made Marianne’s face harden afterward.

Children surviving in dangerous places learned quickly which adults felt wrong.

The final peaceful night of Rayden’s childhood ended beside the stove.

The children slept beneath patched blankets while snow drifted softly outside the cracked windows.

Caelum practiced tiny sparks of magic between his fingers.

Garrick sharpened wooden practice swords beside the table.

Marianne repaired winter coats beneath candlelight.

And Rayden — eleven years old, bruised, angry, alive — slowly fell asleep believing the orphanage would still exist tomorrow.

He was wrong.

II. Lord Halbrecht Veyne

Sundered Age — Year 1031

Children learned fear before they learned language in Oaksmane.

Not dramatic fear.

Not battlefield fear.

Smaller things.

The sound of boots stopping outside your alley at night.

The smell of alcohol on wealthy men.

The silence adults used when speaking about nobles.

Rayden learned all of those before turning eleven.

Lord Halbrecht Veyne taught him the rest.

To the upper districts, Halbrecht was admired.

The newspapers called him:

“The Lantern of Oaksmane.”

He funded orphanages.

Donated grain during famine winters.

Sponsored church restoration projects.

Paid for military widows’ funerals.

His portrait hung inside government halls.

People described him as: cultured, soft-spoken, disciplined, generous.

Rich districts loved men like Halbrecht because they turned cruelty into paperwork.

The lower districts knew different stories.

Girls disappearing from dockside streets.

Young servants transferred quietly between noble estates.

Soldiers beaten half-dead after questioning orders.

No one said Halbrecht’s name loudly when discussing such things.

Because poor people survived longer by pretending not to notice monsters.

Garrick noticed everything.

That was the problem.

One freezing evening after military rotation, Garrick arrived at the orphanage later than usual with fresh bruises across his jaw and dried blood staining the collar of his uniform.

Marianne immediately pulled him into the kitchen after the children slept.

Rayden overheard part of the conversation through the hallway.

Not intentionally.

The walls were simply thin.

“You fought again.”

Garrick sat heavily beside the stove.

“He deserved worse.”

Marianne cleaned blood from his cheek carefully.

“You’ll get yourself killed.”

Garrick laughed quietly.

Not because anything was funny.

Because exhausted people sometimes laughed instead of screaming.

“You know what one of Halbrecht’s officers did today?”

Marianne said nothing.

Garrick stared into the fire.

“A girl stole bread near the south market.”

“She couldn’t have been older than fourteen.”

“They broke three fingers teaching her discipline.”

The room stayed quiet for several seconds afterward.

Then Garrick spoke again.

“He watched it happen.”

“Smiling.”

Marianne closed her eyes slowly.

Like someone hearing confirmation of a fear they already carried.

“You need to leave his service.”

Garrick immediately shook his head.

“And go where?”

“You think soldiers like me retire?”

“You think poor men get choices?”

His voice lowered afterward.

“If I disappear, this place loses half its winter food.”

Marianne looked around the orphanage kitchen.

The cracked walls.

The ration shelves.

The sleeping children beyond the hallway.

She hated that he was right.

Later that night, Garrick found Rayden awake pretending to sleep near the stove.

Without speaking, Garrick sat beside him and handed over a wrapped piece of sweetbread stolen from military rations.

Rayden accepted it silently.

After a while he asked:

“Why don’t you kill him?”

Garrick became very still.

The fire crackled softly between them.

Finally:

“Because men like Halbrecht never stand alone.”

Rayden frowned.

Garrick looked toward the dark windows.

“You kill one monster wearing silk, another one inherits the chair before the body gets cold.”

That answer stayed with Rayden for years.

Halbrecht visited the orphanage again three days later.

The entire building changed when his carriage stopped outside.

Children became quieter.

Marianne’s shoulders stiffened.

Even Garrick’s jaw tightened visibly.

Fear moved through rooms faster than smoke.

Halbrecht entered wearing dark winter velvet trimmed with silver embroidery.

Snow melted slowly across his polished boots while two armed guards remained near the doorway.

One carried a military saber.

The other wore black battlemage markings stitched into the collar of his coat.

The mage was young.

Arrogant.

Too comfortable holding power.

Small blue sparks occasionally danced unconsciously across his fingertips.

The younger children stared at him with fascination.

Caelum did not.

Caelum looked terrified.

Rayden noticed immediately.

Caelum had gone pale beside the staircase.

“What’s wrong?”

Caelum stared toward Halbrecht without blinking.

“Something’s wrong around him.”

Rayden frowned.

“What does that mean?”

Caelum swallowed hard.

“I don’t know.”

“It feels…”

“Cold.”

Then the candle beside them suddenly extinguished itself.

Halbrecht moved through the orphanage smiling politely while Marianne guided him room to room.

He complimented the children.

Praised the discipline.

Asked harmless questions softly.

Everything about him sounded civilized.

That was what made him terrifying.

Monsters shouting about cruelty were easy to recognize.

Halbrecht spoke gently while making children afraid to breathe.

At one point he stopped near the older girls repairing winter coats beside the stove.

One girl lowered her eyes immediately.

Another subtly shifted farther away.

Halbrecht smiled warmly.

“You work very hard here.”

His hand rested briefly against one girl’s shoulder.

She visibly flinched.

The noble noticed.

And smiled slightly wider.

Rayden saw Garrick’s hand tighten hard enough around a chair behind them that the wood creaked.

Marianne noticed too.

She stepped between Halbrecht and the girls smoothly enough to appear accidental.

“The younger children prepared tea.”

Halbrecht’s eyes rested on her for several seconds afterward.

Not angry.

Interested.

That look frightened Rayden more than shouting would have.

Lord Halbrecht Veyne · The Lantern of Oaksmane

The battlemage guard wandered near the training yard afterward while Garrick supervised practice drills with the boys.

The mage watched them with amused contempt.

“Training them for war already?”

Garrick ignored him.

The mage smiled.

Blue sparks rolled lazily between his fingers.

“You know what happens to most slum boys in military service?”

“They die before twenty.”

Garrick finally looked at him then.

“And yet you’re still talking.”

The mage laughed.

But his hand drifted closer toward the spell focus attached near his belt.

For a moment Rayden genuinely thought Garrick might kill him right there in the yard.

Part of him wanted Garrick to.

That night, after Halbrecht finally left, Marianne locked every door personally.

Twice.

Then checked the upstairs windows.

Then the cellar entrance.

Rayden watched silently from the staircase.

She was afraid.

Not nervous.

Afraid.

Adults rarely allowed children to see that openly.

Garrick found her beside the stove after midnight.

Snow hammered softly against the windows outside.

The kitchen fire burned low.

For a long time neither spoke.

Finally Marianne whispered:

“He’s going to come back.”

Garrick said nothing.

Because they both already knew she was right.

Marianne stared into the fire.

“I see the way he looks at them.”

Garrick’s jaw tightened slowly.

“If he touches any of the children…”

He stopped speaking halfway through the sentence.

Because both of them understood how that sentence ended.

Not with survival.

Marianne reached across the table and held his hand tightly.

“Don’t throw your life away.”

Garrick looked at her quietly.

Tired.

Angry.

In love.

Then he said the thing that would eventually destroy all of them:

“Some things deserve violence.”

III. The Night of Fire

Sundered Age — Year 1031

The storm arrived just after midnight.

Rain hammered the rooftops of Oaksmane hard enough to shake loose old wood from the factory walls, and thunder rolled across the district like distant artillery.

Most of the orphanage children slept downstairs near the stove because the upper floor had become too cold to survive through the night comfortably.

Blankets overlapped across the floor.

Tiny bodies slept shoulder-to-shoulder for warmth.

The fire crackled softly inside the iron stove.

For a few brief hours, the orphanage almost felt peaceful.

Marianne sat beside the kitchen table repairing winter coats beneath candlelight while rain tapped steadily against the windows.

Caelum sat nearby with two younger boys watching tiny sparks dance between his fingertips.

The younger children giggled quietly every time the sparks changed color.

Rayden sat cross-legged near the stove sharpening old wooden practice swords using Garrick’s whetstone despite knowing the stone was meant for steel.

“You’re ruining it.”

Caelum grinned without looking up.

Rayden shrugged.

“It already looks ruined.”

Marianne smiled faintly from the table.

Small moments like that mattered more in Oaksmane than people realized.

Poor children learned to treasure peace because it never stayed long.

Garrick was gone.

Military rotation under Halbrecht’s command.

Rayden hated those nights most.

The orphanage always felt less safe without him there.

Even Marianne seemed quieter whenever Garrick was away.

Thunder shook the windows again.

One of the younger girls stirred nervously in her sleep.

Marianne stood, walked across the room, and gently pulled the blanket higher around the child’s shoulders.

Rayden watched her for a moment.

Then the knocking started.

Three slow knocks against the front door.

Not requests.

Ownership.

The entire orphanage became still.

Even the storm suddenly sounded distant.

Marianne froze beside the doorway.

Rayden saw fear enter her face immediately.

Real fear.

Not concern.

Not nervousness.

Fear.

The knocking came again.

Slow.

Patient.

Confident.

Marianne quietly slipped one hand beneath her apron before approaching the door.

Rayden noticed the kitchen knife immediately.

She had been expecting this.

When Marianne finally opened the door, cold rain and torchlight spilled into the orphanage hall.

Lord Halbrecht Veyne stood outside beneath a black umbrella held by one of his guards.

Two soldiers waited behind him.

The battlemage from earlier visits stood nearest the stairs, blue sparks crawling lazily between his fingers.

Halbrecht smiled warmly.

“My apologies for the hour.”

“The storm became unpleasant.”

Marianne did not move aside.

“The children are sleeping.”

Halbrecht’s expression remained calm.

“Then I’ll be brief.”

Without waiting for permission, he stepped inside.

The guards followed immediately.

One quietly shut the door behind them.

The battlemage traced glowing symbols briefly near the frame.

The lock clicked by itself.

Caelum went pale instantly.

“Rayden...”

“They locked it.”

Rayden’s stomach tightened.

Halbrecht slowly removed his gloves while walking deeper into the orphanage.

He complimented the repairs.

Asked if the children were fed properly.

Spoke softly enough that, without context, he might have sounded kind.

That was what made him terrifying.

Everything cruel about him wore a civilized face first.

The older girls near the stove lowered their heads immediately when Halbrecht approached.

One visibly shifted backward.

Another grabbed the sleeve of the younger child beside her protectively.

Halbrecht noticed every reaction.

And enjoyed it.

“You work very hard here.”

His hand rested against one older girl’s shoulder.

She flinched hard enough to nearly drop the sewing basket in her lap.

Halbrecht smiled slightly wider.

“There’s no reason to be frightened.”

Marianne moved immediately between them.

“They’re children.”

For the first time that night, Halbrecht’s eyes changed.

The warmth disappeared.

Not anger.

Something colder.

“You forget who funds this building.”

Marianne did not move.

“No.”

“I remember exactly.”

The room became dangerously quiet.

Even the guards looked tense now.

Rayden saw one of them glance nervously toward Halbrecht.

The noble slowly stepped closer to Marianne.

“You should be careful how you speak to your betters.”

Then his eyes drifted back toward the girls.

“Especially when they depend on generosity.”

One of the older girls tried quietly moving away.

Halbrecht grabbed her wrist immediately.

She cried out in fear.

Rayden moved before thinking.

He grabbed the nearest wooden practice sword and charged.

“LET HER GO!”

The wooden blade struck Halbrecht across the shoulder hard enough to crack loudly.

The room froze.

For half a second nobody moved.

Then the battlemage laughed.

“The little animal bites.”

Halbrecht slowly turned toward Rayden.

The civilized mask was gone now.

Only contempt remained.

One of the guards struck Rayden across the face hard enough to throw him against the table.

Caelum screamed.

The younger children woke instantly in panic.

Everything shattered afterward.

Marianne moved first.

The kitchen knife flashed from beneath her apron and buried itself into a guard’s throat.

Hot blood exploded across the stove and floorboards.

The man collapsed choking violently while trying to pull the blade free.

Everyone stared at Marianne in shock.

Including Halbrecht.

For one glorious second, the terrified caretaker of the orphanage looked like a cornered wolf willing to die with its teeth buried in something important.

“GET AWAY FROM THEM!”

The battlemage reacted instantly.

Blue fire erupted across his hands.

He hurled ignition magic into the curtains beside the staircase.

The old dry fabric exploded into flame immediately.

Another spell struck the shelves near the kitchen.

Oil lamps shattered.

Fire raced across the floorboards.

The storm wind pouring through cracked windows fed the flames hungrily.

Within seconds the orphanage became an inferno.

Children screamed everywhere.

Smoke rolled downward across the ceiling thick enough to choke on.

The younger boys panicked immediately, trampling blankets while trying to run toward the locked front door.

One slammed against it desperately.

It did not move.

The battlemage smiled.

Chaos consumed the orphanage.

One guard grabbed an older girl by the hair and dragged her screaming toward the hallway wall.

Another overturned tables searching for hiding children.

The fire spread unnaturally fast now, climbing walls and beams like living things.

Smoke burned Rayden’s lungs.

He could barely see.

Caelum raised trembling hands instinctively.

Ice exploded across the nearby floorboards in a desperate uncontrolled burst of magic, slowing part of the flames long enough for several younger children to escape toward the pantry corridor.

The battlemage noticed immediately.

“Well.”

“A gifted one.”

Blue fire gathered in his palms.

Caelum froze in terror.

Rayden tackled the mage before the spell released fully.

The half-formed fire blast detonated sideways into the ceiling beams instead.

Burning timber crashed downward between Rayden and Caelum in an explosion of sparks.

Children screamed behind the collapsing debris.

Rayden tried reaching through the flames.

“CAELUM!”

Smoke swallowed the answer.

Then another section of the ceiling collapsed between them.

Rayden never saw him again that night.

The Night of Fire · Oaksmane · Year 1031

Marianne fought like someone who already understood she would die there.

She smashed boiling soup across one guard’s face.

Stabbed another through the side of the neck with broken kitchen steel.

Dragged terrified children toward collapsing exits while smoke blackened her lungs.

One little girl became trapped beneath a fallen beam near the staircase.

Marianne tried lifting it alone despite the flames climbing her sleeves.

She could have escaped then.

Instead she stayed.

The battlemage kicked Rayden hard enough to crack ribs.

Blue fire gathered again around his hands.

“You should’ve stayed quiet.”

Then the front doors exploded inward.

Garrick arrived like violence given human form.

Rain and smoke burst around him as he charged through the doorway carrying military steel still wet from the storm outside.

He saw the burning orphanage.

The screaming children.

Marianne covered in blood.

Rayden half-buried beneath debris.

And something inside him broke permanently.

Garrick Cale · Into the Fire

The first guard died before turning fully around.

Garrick’s sword entered through the man’s mouth and exited behind the skull.

The second lost his arm at the elbow.

The battlemage barely managed to raise a spell barrier before Garrick slammed into him hard enough to drive both men through a burning table.

Blue fire erupted wildly across the room.

Garrick beat the mage to death against the floorboards with such brutality that Rayden barely recognized him anymore.

Then Garrick saw Marianne.

Still alive.

Barely.

Burns covered one side of her body.

Blood soaked her dress.

Smoke trembled in every breath.

Yet she still shielded trapped children behind her.

Garrick stared at her in horror.

Marianne looked back at him through the flames.

“Save them.”

Not herself.

Them.

Sister Marianne · Save Them

The ceiling groaned overhead.

Burning beams began collapsing across the orphanage.

Garrick dragged Rayden upright while children screamed somewhere beyond the smoke-choked hallways.

Rayden kept searching desperately through the fire.

“CAELUM!”

“CAELUM!”

No answer came.

Only fire.

Garrick tried reaching Marianne.

Half the upper floor collapsed between them first.

Burning debris exploded across the room.

The trapped children screamed once.

Then stopped.

Marianne disappeared behind the flames.

Garrick screamed her name exactly once.

Rayden never forgot the sound.

It did not sound human.

Garrick carried Rayden through the pantry corridor moments before the orphanage roof collapsed completely.

The sound thundered across Oaksmane.

Burning sparks spiraled upward into the storm sky like dying stars.

The screams inside stopped one by one.

Then silence remained.

Lord Halbrecht Veyne survived.

That was the cruelest part.

Monsters like him usually did.

IV. The Execution

Sundered Age — Year 1031

The official report described the orphanage fire as an accident caused by unstable heating lanterns during the storm.

Twenty-three children dead.

Structural collapse.

Tragic circumstances.

No mention of soldiers.

No mention of screaming girls dragged through smoke.

No mention of magical fire.

No mention of Lord Halbrecht Veyne.

Oaksmane returned to normal frighteningly fast.

The factories reopened by sunrise.

The canals still carried blood-colored waste.

The rich districts still held parties uphill while smoke from the orphanage ruins drifted through the lower streets.

Poor people learned early that the world did not stop for their dead.

Rayden woke three days later inside a rotting storage room behind an abandoned butcher shop.

Every breath hurt.

The battlemage’s kick had cracked ribs.

Burns covered one side of his arm.

The smoke damage inside his lungs made coughing feel like swallowing broken glass.

Fever came and went constantly.

Sometimes he woke screaming because he smelled smoke again.

Sometimes he heard children crying inside walls that no longer existed.

Sometimes he saw Marianne burning every time he closed his eyes.

Garrick stayed beside him almost the entire time.

He barely slept.

Barely spoke.

Barely moved unless Rayden’s fever worsened.

The man who once laughed with orphan children now looked like something hollowed out from the inside.

One night Rayden woke to the sound of Garrick vomiting outside the room.

Not ordinary sickness.

The violent kind.

The kind soldiers developed after seeing things they could not survive mentally.

Rayden quietly watched through the cracked doorway while Garrick knelt in the alley rain with bloodshot eyes and shaking hands.

For the first time in his life, Garrick looked weak.

That frightened Rayden more than the fire had.

Later that night Garrick sat beside him in silence while sharpening his military sword beneath lantern light.

The blade still carried dried blood inside the fuller groove.

Rayden stared at it for a long time.

“You killed them.”

Garrick did not answer immediately.

Finally:

“Not enough.”

Rayden asked the question anyway.

“Did you find Caelum?”

Silence.

Garrick looked away first.

That answer hurt worse than words.

The next morning Garrick marched directly into the Oaksmane district barracks carrying Marianne’s bloodstained kitchen knife wrapped in cloth.

Rayden tried following him despite the fever.

Garrick stopped him near the alley entrance.

“Stay here.”

“He killed them.”

Garrick’s face hardened.

“I know.”

“Then kill him.”

For a moment something dangerous moved behind Garrick’s eyes.

Then it disappeared.

“If I kill him now, they’ll bury the truth with me.”

“I’m doing this properly first.”

That sentence haunted Rayden later.

Because it was the last time Garrick still believed justice and law were connected.

The accusation became public before noon.

Witnesses later described Garrick standing inside the district command hall soaked from rainwater while openly naming Lord Halbrecht responsible for the orphanage massacre.

Soldiers went silent.

Officers stopped writing.

Even servants froze.

Because everyone in that room believed him.

That was the problem.

Halbrecht arrived less than an hour later.

Perfectly composed.

Fresh gloves.

Clean uniform coat.

The burn mark across his wrist from Rayden’s wooden sword strike had already been hidden beneath silk wrappings.

He looked like a man attending business negotiations.

Not someone who had watched children burn alive days earlier.

Garrick attacked him before the hearing even began.

Not recklessly.

Not emotionally.

Like an execution.

He crossed the command hall in three steps and slammed Halbrecht against the stone wall hard enough to crack plaster.

The noble’s guards reacted instantly.

Steel erupted across the room.

Garrick broke one man’s jaw with the hilt of his sword before six soldiers dragged him to the ground.

Even restrained, he kept trying to reach Halbrecht.

“You burned children.”

Halbrecht calmly adjusted his gloves.

“You are emotional, Sergeant Cale.”

Garrick nearly broke free after that.

The trial lasted less than two hours.

No orphan survivors were questioned.

No investigation occurred.

No evidence was reviewed.

Marianne was officially recorded as mentally unstable.

The fire became an unfortunate accident.

Garrick became the criminal.

The charges included:

  • Assault against noble authority
  • Treasonous conduct
  • Incitement of unrest
  • Attempted murder of a district lord

The sentence was immediate execution.

Rayden learned about it from a drunk dockworker near sunset.

At first he refused to believe him.

Then he started running.

The fever still burned through his body.

His ribs screamed every time he inhaled.

But Rayden sprinted through Oaksmane anyway, slipping through rainwater and muddy streets while desperately trying to reach the execution square.

On the way he begged anyone armed for help.

Mercenaries.

Guards.

Dock fighters.

Most laughed.

One mercenary looked at the execution order and quietly handed it back.

“That name’s protected, kid.”

Another said:

“You don’t survive fighting nobles.”

Rayden hated adults after that.

The execution platform stood at the center of Oaksmane’s lower market square beside the frozen fountain.

Snow drifted lightly through the evening air.

Hundreds gathered to watch.

Public executions were not punishment.

They were reminders.

The executioner waiting beside the block was enormous.

An orc.

Nearly seven feet tall with gray-green skin covered in ritual scar patterns burned into the flesh years earlier.

His shoulders looked broad enough to carry doors as shields.

The execution axe resting beside him resembled a siege weapon more than a tool.

Its blade was stained dark from old blood no amount of sharpening ever fully removed.

The crowd whispered his title quietly:

“Gorvak the Silent.”

The orc never spoke.

He simply performed executions because the district paid well and dead men did not care what race held the axe.

Garrick arrived in chains shortly after dusk.

Rayden almost failed to recognize him.

They had beaten him badly.

One eye swollen shut.

Split lips.

Bruised ribs visible even beneath torn clothing.

But he still walked upright.

That mattered.

Halbrecht watched from an elevated balcony wrapped in heavy winter furs while wine steamed gently beside him.

Warm.

Comfortable.

Protected.

That image stayed inside Rayden forever.

Power watching suffering from safety.

The magistrate began reading the charges while snow gathered slowly across the execution platform.

His voice sounded bored.

Like condemning innocent men had become routine years earlier.

Maybe it had.

Rayden forced himself through the crowd desperately.

“GARRICK!”

People shoved him backward immediately.

Someone struck him across the shoulder.

Another voice snapped:

“Shut the boy up.”

Garrick heard him anyway.

For the first time since the orphanage fire, Garrick smiled.

Small.

Broken.

Tired.

But real.

Then he shook his head once.

Don’t.

Rayden understood immediately.

Garrick was not afraid of dying.
