I. The Kingdom in Peacetime

Saltmere · Vaelmark · Ironmane Fields · Year 1049

The kingdom was working.

Not dramatically. Not in the way that things working in the years before had been dramatic — the specific drama of something held together by will and insufficient resources, the constant recalculation of what could be asked of people who were already giving more than was fair to ask.

In Year 1049, things were simply working.

The six railway lines ran. The twenty-two rift gates held. The Dawnfleet was operational at Saltmere. The Ironpass Citadel had been complete for under a year — finished in Year 1048 — and its first full garrison winter had passed without incident. The treasury produced the specific number that Astrya Lenn had projected in Year 1047, which she had known it would and which still produced, on the morning it was confirmed, the specific expression she wore when accuracy met prediction and both turned out to be the same thing.

Tiber Oswick had predicted the treasury number four months earlier.

He had not told Astrya.

This was their arrangement.

The ministers settled into their titles the way people settled into things that had always been true and had simply been given names. Senna Ash continued recording everything with the same precision she had applied before anyone called her Chief Archivist. Cyrus Vexley continued improving infrastructure that was already functional because the functional version had not yet reached its optimal version, and the gap between the two was Cyrus's permanent operational context. These two facts were related.

Rufus filed this under: occupational continuity. Roen filed it under: more floor to walk on.

"It's better."

"Yes."

Crest said nothing further.

The Greywood's own borderlands had carried traces of it in Year 1047, before he relocated the deer herds, before he spent two years coaxing the affected root systems back toward health. He knew what it felt like at the edges — faint, treatable, a wound that could still close.

This was not the edge of anything.

This was the place where something had been present long enough that the ground had stopped fighting it and started simply living around it — the specific resignation of a living system that had tried to heal a wound for as long as healing was possible and had then, gradually, without any single moment of decision, stopped trying.

He stood.

Grond was looking at him.

"Yeah,"

Thorin said.

"I feel it too."

He climbed back onto Grond's shoulders.

They continued northwest.

The dead zones became visible after another mile.

Not large. Patches — areas of ground perhaps thirty feet across where the highland scrub grass had stopped growing entirely, the bare earth beneath exposed in the specific way of soil that had given up on supporting anything. Around the edges of each patch, the surviving vegetation showed the particular stress pattern of plants that were drawing water and nutrients from ground that no longer wanted to provide them — stunted, the leaves a wrong shade of yellow-green, growing away from the dead patches rather than toward them.

Thorin counted eleven patches in the next two miles.

He stopped counting after that.

At the edge of the twelfth patch, he found the first body.

It had been an orc.

Highland build — the broader shoulders and shorter limbs of the Stormmassif bloodlines, distinct from the lowland orc communities Thorin had encountered in Coldmarch and Goldgate. Armor of hammered iron and hide, old but maintained, the specific care of someone who had kept their equipment in working order for a long time because the alternative was not surviving.

The body had been dead for weeks.

What had killed it was visible even at this distance, even after weeks — a network of black lines spreading from a wound at the shoulder, branching outward across what remained of the exposed skin in the specific pattern that Thorin recognized from Velkan's research notes, from Zareth's field reports, from the description that Seraphine had given the Stone Table eighteen months ago of what Void corruption did to living tissue when it was given time to work.

The wound itself was small.

A claw mark. A single deep gouge at the shoulder — the kind of injury that, on its own, an orc this size would have survived easily.

The corruption had done the rest.

Twenty feet away, a second body. A goblin, smaller, the same black branching pattern radiating from a wound at the leg. Twenty feet beyond that, a third — and this one was not an orc or a goblin.

It had been something else. Something that, before the corruption, had probably not had a name that any lowland taxonomy possessed — a creature native to the deep Stormmassif, four-legged, the specific size and build of something that had once hunted in these foothills and had, at some point in the recent past, encountered the contaminated ground and not survived the encounter.

Its corpse was the most thoroughly corrupted of the three.

The black branching pattern had consumed nearly the entire body — not eating it, not in any sense Thorin's experience with predation could classify, but rewriting it, the specific texture of flesh that had been present for the corruption's full duration and had stopped, somewhere in that duration, being flesh in any sense that mattered.

Thorin dismounted again.

He walked the perimeter of the dead patch slowly, reading it the way he read everything — the orc's body near the patch's edge, where the corruption had reached him from a wound sustained somewhere else and he had walked here to die, or had been walking away from here when the corruption finished its work. The goblin closer to the center. The unidentified creature at the patch's heart, where the ground itself was most thoroughly wrong.

A story, told in the only language the dead could still speak.

Something — the creature — had been here first. Had encountered the corrupted ground, or had been corrupted by something else and come here to die, or had simply been an animal in a place that became wrong while it was standing in it. It had died. The corruption had not stopped with its death; it had continued working through the body, and the ground beneath the body, expanding the dead patch a little further than it had been before.

The goblin had found it. Maybe scavenging — the highland goblin clans were resourceful, and a fresh corpse in lean country was not something to walk past. The goblin had touched it, or been close enough, or simply stayed too long in ground that was already wrong before the corpse arrived. The corruption had found a new host.

The orc had found the goblin.

Maybe trying to help. Maybe trying to retrieve a clan member's body. Maybe simply walking the same patrol route the goblin had walked, encountering the same wrongness that the goblin had already succumbed to, and receiving from it the same gift.

Three deaths. One patch of ground. The corruption growing a little with each one — not through violence, not through any act that a living creature had chosen, but through the simple mechanism of contact and time, the specific horror of a danger that did not attack and did not need to.

Thorin had read enough.

He stood at the patch's edge for a long moment.

He thought about the Greywood. About the deer he had relocated in Year 1047, the borderland root systems he had spent two years coaxing back toward health, the specific luxury of having caught it early enough that healing had still been possible.

He thought about how many patches like this one existed between here and wherever the contamination's edge actually was.

He thought about the third Ridgerunner message — the thing moving toward you moved us first — and understood, for the first time with the specific clarity of direct evidence rather than data on a map, exactly what that sentence had meant.

He climbed back onto Grond.

They continued northwest.

Gorvak Doomhide was waiting at the meeting point.

He had arrived first.

Thorin understood, the moment he saw the troll standing in the center of the agreed clearing, that this was not an accident and was not aggression — it was simply how Gorvak had operated for four hundred years. Arrive before the other party. Let them walk into your space rather than the reverse. Make the first assessment from the position of someone who was already settled, watching someone else arrive.

He was alone.

No commanders. No guard. No retinue of any kind — which was, Thorin understood immediately, its own kind of message. A four-hundred-year-old troll did not need a guard at a meeting he had chosen the ground for. The absence of one was not carelessness. It was confidence stated without words.

He was exactly as the descriptions had suggested and larger than any description could fully convey.

Nine, perhaps ten feet at full height — though he was not standing at full height, Thorin registered, he was standing in the specific settled posture of something that had been waiting long enough to stop holding itself at attention. Granite-grey skin, deeply furrowed, the texture of stone that had weathered four centuries of whatever the Stormmassif had to offer and had not cracked. Small fire-orange horns, curved inward, worn smooth at the tips. A single golden pauldron on the left shoulder — spiked, tarnished, clearly taken rather than made. A dark weapon at his side, shorter than his reach would have suggested necessary, the specific economy of something that had learned long ago that reach was not the variable that mattered.

His eyes found Thorin and Grond as they entered the clearing.

He did not move.

He did not speak.

Thorin dismounted. Grond settled onto his haunches at a respectful distance — close enough to be present, far enough to signal that this was a conversation and not a confrontation.

The silence continued.

Thorin had expected this. He had spent enough time around things that had been alive for a very long time — the Greywood itself, in its way, was older than any individual measure of years could capture — to recognize the specific quality of a silence that was not hostile, simply unhurried. Gorvak was reading him. Taking the time the reading required. Four hundred years did not produce impatience; it produced the opposite, the specific patience of something that had outlived every reason to rush toward a conclusion.

Thorin let him take the time.

When Gorvak finally spoke, his voice carried the specific quality that Thorin had not expected — not the growl of something performing menace, not the booming authority of a warlord addressing an inferior. It was low, slow, and tired. The voice of something that had said most of what it had to say a very long time ago and had been mostly silent since, and was now speaking again only because the situation required it.

"You came alone."

"So did you."

Gorvak's eyes moved to Grond. Held there for a moment.

"That is not alone."

"No,"

Thorin agreed.

"It isn't."

Something in Gorvak's expression shifted — not quite a smile, the granite-grey features were not built for anything Thorin would have recognized as a smile, but something adjacent to acknowledgment. The specific small recognition of one thing that did not travel alone meeting another thing that did not travel alone.

Thorin did not open with Starhaven.

He had decided this on the ride — not as strategy, though it would function as strategy, but because it was simply true and the truth was the only thing he had brought with him that was worth offering first.

"Three weeks ago the birds in my forest changed their migration."

Gorvak's eyes settled on him fully for the first time.

"A week after that, a deer herd I've been managing for six years relocated itself to the southern quadrant for no reason I could find."

"A few days after that, a beehive that has produced surplus every season for nine years stopped producing."

He paused.

"My forest has been telling me something is coming for three weeks."

"I rode out to find out what."

Gorvak was silent for a long moment.

When he spoke again, something had changed in his voice — not warmth, Thorin would not have called it warmth, but the specific quality of someone who had just heard something they had not expected to hear from this direction.

"Your forest speaks to you."

"It tries."

"I try to listen."

Gorvak looked at the ground beneath them — the same ground Thorin had knelt to feel an hour back, the corroded hum, the second frequency underneath.

"The ground here has been speaking for longer than three weeks."

It was not a question.

"I felt it,"

Thorin said.

"An hour back. And the patches. The bodies."

Gorvak's eyes did not leave him.

"You saw the patches."

"Three of them. An orc, a goblin, and something I didn't recognize."

Something happened in Gorvak's expression that Thorin had not anticipated and would think about for a long time afterward.

It was grief.

Brief — present for less than a second, the specific compression of four hundred years of practice at not showing things, but present, and unmistakable once it had been there.

"The goblin's name was Skreel."

He said it flatly. Not performing the grief — simply stating the fact that the corpse had had a name, because someone should say it, and there was no one else present who would.

"He scouted for the Redspire clan for eleven years. He found water sources in country that should not have had any. He had a mate and two young, both still alive, both walking south with the column right now."

"He found the creature's body four months ago. He thought it was simply dead. He did not know to be afraid of dead things in that country — nothing in his eleven years had taught him that a dead thing could still be dangerous."

A pause.

"The orc was Vrennach. Berserker Clan. He went looking for Skreel when Skreel did not return from a scouting run. He found him three days too late to do anything but die beside him."

Gorvak's eyes moved to the northwest — toward wherever Skreel and Vrennach had died, toward the contamination's edge, toward everything that lay beyond it.

"There are forty more stories like that one between here and where my people used to live."

"I know all of them."

"That is what four hundred years gives you, in the end."

"Not wisdom. Just names."

He looked back at Thorin.

"You are the first creature from the lowlands who has looked at what is behind us and seen it for what it is, rather than seeing us and assuming we brought it."

Thorin was quiet for a moment.

Then:

"I can offer you ground."

He said it plainly. Not as an opening position in a negotiation — as the actual thing he had ridden out here intending to say, the offer he had discussed with the Stone Table before leaving, the genuine possibility that Starhaven's territory, vast and only partially settled, contained room for forty thousand refugees if the kingdom committed to making it so.

"It would take time to arrange properly. Food, water, shelter at the scale you'd need — that's not something that exists today. But it could exist. We have built things from less than that before."

Gorvak listened.

He did not interrupt.

When Thorin finished, Gorvak was quiet for longer than he had been quiet at any point in the conversation so far.

"You mean it."

"Yes."

"I believe you do."

He looked at the dead patch of ground visible at the clearing's edge — the same wrongness, the same corroded hum, present here too.

"You don't understand what is holding them together."

Thorin waited.

"Forty thousand. Eleven clans. Some of those clans have been at war with each other longer than your kingdom has existed. The Ironjaw and the Redspire fought for forty years over passes that don't matter anymore because the passes are gone — swallowed by what's behind us. I told them to stop."

He paused.

"They stopped."

"Not because I am wise. Not because I am loved. I am not loved. I am four hundred years old and I have outlived everyone who might have loved me and most of what they would have recognized as reasons to."

"They stopped because I am the thing that has survived longer than anything else they know of, and following the thing that survives is the only law the Stormmassif has ever actually enforced."

He looked directly at Thorin.

"I am not their king. I am their direction."

"Eleven clans, forty years of grievance between several of them, walking the same road because the road is the only thing they currently agree on."

"If I tell them to stop here — if there is suddenly a place to be, instead of a direction to go—"

He did not finish the sentence.

He did not need to.

"The grievances do not go away because the road stops. They were always there. The road was just louder."

"Take away the road, and in three days you are not facing one army at your border."

"You are standing in the middle of six wars that have been waiting forty years to restart, with forty thousand people and nowhere for any of them to go, and the contamination still behind all of it, still spreading, because nothing about stopping here makes it stop."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Your offer is real. I believe that. It is also the worst possible thing that could happen to either of us."

Thorin absorbed this.

He had not expected to be told no in a way that made the no feel like the only honest answer available.

"Then what happens."

Gorvak looked toward the southeast — toward the Greywood, toward Greenveil, toward everything Thorin had ridden out from.

"What happens is that the road continues. It has to. And your forest is on it."

"I am not glad of this."

"I want you to understand that I am not glad of this."

"But I have spent four hundred years choosing the option that keeps the largest number of the people following me alive for the longest amount of time, and every version of that calculation I have run — and I have run all of them, dwarf, in the weeks since I knew your forest was in the road — ends the same way."

He looked back at Thorin.

"You will fight us."

"I do not think you will lose easily. I have heard what your forest can do. I believe most of it."

"But I cannot choose not to send them forward, because forward is the only direction that keeps them together, and together is the only thing keeping six wars from happening on top of everything else."

A long silence.

"I am sorry,"

Thorin said.

He meant it.

Gorvak looked at him for a long moment.

"I believe that too."

They stood in the clearing for a while without speaking.

The dead patch at the edge hummed its wrong frequency. Grond, behind Thorin, had not moved — but his eyes had not left the patch since they entered the clearing, the specific watchfulness of an animal that understood, in whatever way animals understood such things, that the ground itself had become something to be wary of.

Gorvak spoke again, and this time something in his voice had changed — quieter, the specific register of a question that had been sitting unasked for a long time and was finally being given the chance.

"Your forest. The one that speaks to you."

"Yes."

"Do you think it will still be there in four hundred years?"

Thorin looked at him.

"Regardless of what happens between us. Regardless of who is left to remember any of this. Do you think the forest will still be standing?"

It was not a tactical question.

It was not a threat, or a test, or anything that belonged to the conversation they had been having.

It was the question of something that had outlived nearly everything it had ever cared about, standing in ground that had been killed slowly over months by something it could not fight directly, asking — genuinely, simply — whether the one thing in this conversation that had seemed, to him, actually alive in the way his homeland used to be alive, would survive long enough to still be alive when he was not.

Thorin thought about the Greywood.

About the root networks he had spent seven years building relationships with. About the deer herds, the beehive, Grond, the way the forest had told him three weeks ago that something was coming because it had been paying attention the whole time, the way it always paid attention.

He thought about the contamination, spreading north, the way it had spread through the Stormmassif and killed Skreel and Vrennach and forty other people whose names Gorvak carried and would carry until he didn't anymore.

"I don't know,"

Thorin said.

The honest answer.

"I'm going to try to make sure it does."

Gorvak held his eyes for a moment.

"That is more than most things say."

He turned.

No further words. No formal closing, no handshake — the specific absence of ceremony from something that had stopped needing ceremony a long time ago and had found that conversations simply ended when they were finished, regardless of whether anything had been resolved.

He walked back toward the northwest at the same unhurried pace he had arrived with.

Thorin watched him go.

He stood in the clearing for a long time after Gorvak had passed out of sight — longer than the ride back required, longer than was strictly necessary for anything except the specific human need to sit with something before carrying it back to people who would need him to have already processed it.

He took the resonance shard from his pack.

The piece of calibrated crystal that Velkan's network maintained — smaller than the projection crystals at Learnstead and the Arcanum, but tuned to the same frequency, capable of relaying a message to the nearest fixed node in the network. The Greenveil forward post carried one. So did Thorin's pack, on this specific ride, because Velkan had insisted on it three weeks ago and Thorin had not argued.

He activated it.

The relay took a moment — the specific brief delay of a signal finding its path through the network's architecture, Greenveil to Vaelmark to Starhold, the same infrastructure that had carried a coronation broadcast to every city simultaneously now carrying four sentences from a clearing in the foothills to a war room.

Thorin spoke into it.

"He's not wrong."

"Neither am I."

"It happens anyway."

A pause.

"I know how he'll commit his army now. I'll explain when I'm back. Start the staging."

He deactivated the shard.

He climbed onto Grond.

Behind them, the dead patch of ground continued its wrong, corroded hum — the same frequency that ran, Thorin now understood with the full weight of having felt it himself, through hundreds of miles of highland stone, through the roots of a homeland that forty thousand people had already lost, toward a forest that was, for now, still alive, and that he intended to keep that way for as long as the ground would let him.

They rode back toward the Greywood.

The way-markers, somewhere behind them in the Greypeak foothills, continued their two-hundred-and-thirty-year-old alarm into the gathering dark.

IV. The Road and What Follows It

Greenveil Staging Ground · The Highland Column · Year 1049

Thorin did not speak again until they reached the staging ground.

Grond carried him the whole way at the steady pace that had brought them out, and Thorin let the silence sit, because the silence was doing something — processing, in the slow way that processing happened when what needed to be processed was not information but the specific weight of having stood close enough to something dying to feel it.

He could still feel it.

Not on his skin — beneath it, the same place his connection to the Greywood lived, the place that had spent forty-one years learning to read what living ground felt like and had spent the last hour learning what dying ground felt like from the inside of a conversation with something that was dying alongside it.

He did not think Gorvak knew.

He thought, turning it over the way he turned over root patterns and soil compositions, that Gorvak might have stopped being able to tell the difference between the two kinds of tired a long time ago — the tired of four hundred years, and the tired of something working on you slowly enough that by the time it was far enough along to notice, noticing didn't change anything.

He filed it.

He did not yet have anywhere to put it.

Drogar Stonefang was waiting at the staging ground when they arrived.

Not inside the perimeter — at its edge, in the open ground between the Greenveil forward positions and wherever his own force was currently holding. Three figures with him, no more. Black stone plate, ancient runes, the specific stillness of soldiers who had been standing in one place long enough that standing had stopped being an effort.

The Ancestor Guard.

Thorin understood, looking at the three of them and at the empty ground around them, that the rest of the four thousand three hundred were not here, and that this was itself a piece of information — a High Chieftain who brought three of his greatest warriors to a meeting and left the remainder of his army exactly where it had been was a High Chieftain who had already decided where that army needed to be and had not moved it for the meeting's sake.

Drogar was larger than Thorin had expected, even having read the Ridgerunner descriptions. Grey-skinned, the deep mountain grey that was different from the granite-grey of Gorvak's skin — Gorvak's was the grey of stone, Drogar's was the grey of weather, of something that lived at altitude and had adapted to it generation after generation. A warhammer rested head-down against the ground beside him, both hands loosely on its haft, the specific posture of a weapon that was not being held so much as accompanied. A shield on his back carried clan markings — old ones, the kind that had been added to rather than replaced, generations of a family's history layered onto the same surface.

He watched Thorin dismount.

He did not ask how the meeting with Gorvak had gone.

"He offered you something true."

Not a question.

"And you offered him something true."

A pause.

"And neither mattered."

Thorin looked at him.

"You've watched him for a while."

"I have watched him my whole life."

"My clan watched him for three generations before that."

"You learn what he is going to say before he says it. You learn that he means it, every time. That is the part most people don't understand about Gorvak Doomhide. He has never once said something he did not mean."

"It just doesn't change what happens next."

"No."

"It never has."

They walked the perimeter of the staging ground together — Drogar's pace unhurried in a way that was different from Gorvak's unhurriedness, Thorin noted. Gorvak's stillness was the stillness of something that had stopped needing to move quickly because nothing it cared about required speed anymore. Drogar's was the stillness of something that had learned, the hard way, that speed cost more than it returned.

"Three of the eleven clans are going to try to prove something in this fight,"

Drogar said.

He said it the way he said everything — flat, the specific tone of someone reporting weather.

"The Redspire clan has wanted standing in the coalition since the unification. They lost their forty-year war with the Ironjaw without winning it — Gorvak ended it before either side could call it finished. That kind of ending doesn't satisfy anyone. The Redspire have been waiting for an assignment that lets them matter."

"If Gorvak gives the order for a flanking route — and he will, because it is the correct order for the terrain you've given him — the Redspire will ask for it before anyone else can."

"Through the forest."

"Through the forest."

Thorin was quiet for a moment.

"How many."

"The Redspire field roughly twenty-six thousand on their own. Gorvak won't send the whole clan — he'll send enough to make the flank credible, and hold the rest in the main column where he can still use them."

He looked toward the Greywood's tree line, visible from the staging ground's western edge as a dark band along the horizon.

"Twenty-eight thousand, would be my estimate. Maybe a little under."

Thorin said nothing.

He was thinking about the root networks. About the seven years of conversation he'd had with this specific forest, about what it had agreed to, about what it had been waiting for an occasion to do.

"That's close to what I have room for."

Drogar looked at him.

Something in his expression shifted — not surprise, exactly. The specific recalibration of someone who had been assuming they understood the shape of a plan and had just learned the plan was larger than they'd accounted for.

"Room for."

"The forest doesn't need to fight them."

"It needs to hold them."

He didn't elaborate further. Drogar didn't ask him to. There was a specific quality to how the two of them were talking — neither one explaining more than the other needed, both of them trusting that the gaps would be filled by competence rather than requiring narration.

"Gorvak,"

Drogar said, after a while.

Thorin looked at him.

"That's mine."

He said it without weight — not a claim staked aggressively, simply a fact stated the way facts were stated between people who didn't need to perform their seriousness.

"I know,"

Thorin said.

"Do you understand why."

Thorin thought about it.

About what Gorvak had told him in the clearing — I am not their king, I am their direction. Following the thing that survives is the only law the Stormmassif has ever enforced.

"Because if Starhaven kills him, the highland clans follow whoever's left standing among themselves, and that's chaos. Eleven clans, forty years of grievance, no direction."

"But if one of their own kills him — in front of all of them — and that one is better than Gorvak was —"

"Then the direction doesn't break,"

Drogar finished.

"It just changes who it's pointing at."

He looked at the warhammer resting against his shoulder.

"A dead enemy is useful."

"A dead warrior is expensive."

He said it like he'd said it a thousand times before — because he had, Thorin understood. It wasn't a saying Drogar had adopted. It was a saying Drogar had built his entire life around, the specific philosophy of someone who had learned, watching his clan get destroyed, exactly what it cost to waste a life on a death that didn't need to happen.

"If I kill him cleanly — quickly, no spectacle — the coalition doesn't see anything. They see Gorvak fell. They don't see why it mattered who did it."

"But if it's a fight. A real one. Long enough that they're watching the whole time — long enough that what they see isn't Drogar killed Gorvak, but Drogar earned this —"

"Then when it's over, they don't need to be told who to follow."

"They already watched him become it."

Thorin was quiet for a long moment.

"That's going to cost you."

Drogar's eyes moved to him.

"Everything costs."

"The question is whether it's spent on the right thing."

Before he left, Drogar asked one more thing — and the way he asked it, Thorin understood, was the only moment in the entire conversation where Drogar had let something show that wasn't strategy.

"The clans that follow me after. The ones that didn't choose any of this — the families, the elders, the ones walking behind the war-bands because the column doesn't split."

He paused.

"What happens to them."

Thorin thought about Gorvak's question in the clearing — do you think the forest will still be there in four hundred years — and understood that this was the same question, asked by someone who intended to still be alive to find out the answer.

"There's ground,"

Thorin said.

"In the foothills. Along the Greywood's western edge. It's not contaminated — not yet, not for years at the rate it's spreading, if we can slow it."

"It's not lowland comfort. It's highland ground for highland people."

"But it's safe ground."

"And it would be theirs."

Drogar held his eyes for a moment.

"That's the first time anyone from the lowlands has answered that question without making it sound like charity."

He picked up the warhammer.

"I'll see you on the other side of this, dwarf."

He turned and walked back toward wherever the four thousand three hundred were waiting — the same unhurried pace, the Ancestor Guard falling in behind him without a word, the specific synchrony of soldiers who had walked beside this particular orc for long enough that falling in step required no signal.

The highland column was visible from the Greywood's western ridges three days later.

Visible was the wrong word, in the way that the word was wrong for most things this large. What was visible first was the dust — a haze along the horizon that did not move the way weather moved, too low, too sustained, the specific signature of tens of thousands of feet disturbing ground that had not been disturbed at this scale in longer than the ground could remember.

Then the column itself.

It took most of a day to fully emerge from the foothills, and even then "emerge" understated it — the column did not so much arrive as continue, the leading edge already visible while the rear was still somewhere beyond the horizon, the whole of it moving at the pace of something that had been moving for weeks and intended to keep moving for as long as moving was necessary.

At the front: the Orc Warriors. Heavy infantry, shield and spear, the specific discipline of a formation that had marched together long enough that the marching itself had become a kind of armor — bodies that moved as a single body because moving any other way had stopped being something any individual within it considered.

Ahead of them, spread wide across the column's leading edge: the Goblin Archers. Fast, light, moving in the loose scattered pattern of skirmishers whose job was to be everywhere and nowhere, the eyes of the column, the first warning if the ground ahead held anything the column needed to know about.

Behind the Orc Warriors: the Berserkers.

Kragh Skullsplitter rode at their head — not rode, walked, the same way Gorvak walked, except where Gorvak's walk was patience, Kragh's was barely-contained motion, the specific quality of someone who had been walking at marching pace for weeks and considered every single one of those days an insult to what his body wanted to be doing. His greataxe rode across his back, double-headed, the haft worn smooth from a grip that had not loosened in longer than the weapon had needed sharpening. Blue-skinned, the rare High Orc strain, larger than anything around him except Gorvak himself.

He had heard, two days ago, that the Redspire had been given the flank.

He had not been pleased about it.

He had said so, loudly, to anyone near enough to hear — and Gorvak, walking at the column's center, had not responded, because Gorvak had stopped responding to Kragh's displeasure roughly three decades ago and had found that not responding produced the same result as responding, with considerably less effort.

Behind the Berserkers: the Shieldguards, and at their center, the Lady Orc Matriarch of the Blackfang Clan.

Where Kragh's formation moved like something barely held, the Shieldguards moved like something that had never once needed to be held — spear and shield in overlapping rows, the specific geometry of a defensive formation that doubled as a perimeter, because at its center walked Gorvak, and the Blackfang Clan considered the protection of that center their entire purpose.

The Matriarch had not spoken to Kragh in three days. This was not unusual. She rarely spoke to Kragh at all — she had long since concluded that Kragh's value to the coalition was entirely tactical and that conversation with him produced no return on the time invested. What she had done, in those three days, was reposition two of her Shieldguard companies twice, adjusting their formation depth in response to terrain changes that nobody had asked her to respond to and that, when the column reached ground requiring exactly that adjustment, had already been accounted for.

Gorvak had noticed.

Gorvak noticed everything the Matriarch did, the way he noticed everything that functioned correctly without requiring his attention — which was to say, with the specific quiet approval of someone who had stopped needing to praise things that simply worked.
